# Travel Advisory Hazard Classification Guide

Welcome to the annotation team! This guide outlines the exact rules for labeling advisory sentences in `labeling_canonical.csv`. Please read this document carefully (it takes about 10 minutes) before labeling.

---

### 1. What We Are Labeling and Why

We are building a **Disaster-Aware Travel Itinerary Planner** for 9 tourist destinations in northern Pakistan (*Hunza, Skardu, Gilgit, Naran, Swat, Murree/Galliyat, Chitral, Dir, Neelum Valley*). 

Every day, our backend automatically monitors government hazard advisory PDFs from NDMA and PMD, extracts individual forecast sentences, and uses a machine learning model to classify their severity. In our mobile and web app, your labels directly control what travelers experience:
- **Potential Disruption:** Triggers a **yellow "Be Cautious" notice**. The trip planner applies a subtle background penalty to affected outdoor plans, but suggests no changes.
- **Direct Restriction:** Triggers a **red alert** and displays a **"Repair My Plan" button**. 
- *Crucially:* Our system **never** changes a user's itinerary automatically. Even on red alerts, itineraries only update if the traveler explicitly taps "Repair My Plan" and reviews the proposed alternatives. Accurate labeling ensures travelers are warned of real danger without being panicked by routine mountain weather.

*(Note: "No Impact / Safe" conditions are handled outside the model simply when no active government advisory exists for a city/date. The model only needs to distinguish between the two hazard levels below).*

---

### 2. Important Context Before You Start: Every Sentence Here Is Already "Serious"

NDMA and PMD only publish an advisory when something worth warning about is happening — they never publish a document saying "nothing to report." This means every single sentence you label already comes from a document that was judged important enough to issue.

Because of this, it's easy to unconsciously think: *"this is an official government warning, so it must deserve a red alert."* Resist this. The fact that a sentence appears in an advisory tells you NDMA considered it worth mentioning — it does not tell you whether roads/travel access are affected. In fact, the large majority of sentences in our dataset will correctly be **Class 1 (Potential Disruption)**, because most advisory content describes hazards in general terms (rain, landslide risk, rising river levels) without stating that travel is blocked. That is the normal, expected case — not something to escalate just because it sounds official or urgent.

Ask yourself: *"Setting aside that this is from an official advisory, does THIS SPECIFIC SENTENCE say travel/roads are affected?"* If you remove the "this is government, so it must be serious" feeling and look only at the words, the litmus test in Section 5 still applies exactly as written.

### 3. Understanding the Columns in `labeling_canonical.csv`

When you open the spreadsheet, don't feel overwhelmed by the 15 columns. Most of them are just background tracking data. Here is what every column is for, explained simply:

#### A. The Columns You Fill In (Your Task)
* **`label`**: The final label (**`1`** or **`2`**). For any row that is **not** part of the double-labeling sample, enter your classification directly here.
* **`annotator_1_label`**: Used by **Annotator 1** to enter `1` or `2` for the 130 rows where `is_in_kappa_sample` is `True`.
* **`annotator_2_label`**: Used by **Annotator 2** to enter `1` or `2` independently for the same 130 rows.
* **`label_notes`**: Optional text box. If a sentence was tricky, confusing, or felt like a 50/50 split, write a brief note here (e.g. *"leans 1 because road blockage is not explicitly stated"*).

#### B. The Columns You Read to Make Your Decision
* **`segment_text`**: **The most important column.** This is the exact government advisory sentence you are reading and classifying.
* **`is_in_kappa_sample`**: Shows `True` or `False`.
  * If `True` (130 rows): Annotator 1 and Annotator 2 must **both** label this row separately so we can scientifically measure team agreement (Cohen's Kappa).
  * If `False` (remaining rows): The team can split the work and put the number directly into `label`.
* **`weak_label_hint`**: An automated first-pass guess generated by a simple computer rule (`0`, `1`, or `2`). **Do not blindly follow this**—it only spots basic keywords. Always trust your own human reading and the Litmus Test above all else.
* **`canonical_segment_id`**: A unique ID code for each sentence (e.g. `1XDiROvVKyxetS2aWfQJ_01`). Use this code whenever you want to discuss a specific sentence with your team.

#### C. Background Columns (Purely for Tracking — You Can Ignore These While Labeling)
* **`occurrence_count`**: How many times this identical sentence appeared across different PDFs (e.g. `5` means 5 separate advisories contained this exact sentence).
* **`appears_in_pdfs`**: The list of PDF filenames where this sentence was found.
* **`appears_in_segment_ids`**: The list of all raw segment IDs that share this exact text.
* **`direct_locations`**: Tourist destinations explicitly named in the sentence (e.g. *Swat*, *Murree*).
* **`implied_locations`**: Tourist destinations covered because a wider province was mentioned (e.g. *Khyber Pakhtunkhwa* covers *Naran, Swat, Chitral, Dir*).
* **`matched_region`**: Which province or territory name triggered the implied locations.
* **`location_match_type`**: Shows whether the location match was direct or province-level.

### 4. The Two Severity Classes

In `labeling_canonical.csv`, enter **`1`** for Potential Disruption and **`2`** for Direct Restriction in the `label` column (or your respective `annotator` column).

#### Class 1: POTENTIAL DISRUPTION (`1`)
The sentence describes a natural weather hazard that may occur (rain, snowfall, windstorms, river surges, landslides, avalanches, or localized flooding) **WITHOUT explicitly stating that road or travel access itself is blocked, closed, or disrupted**.
- *"Rains may trigger Landslides / Rockfall in Murree and hilly areas."*
- *"Heavy rains may generate flash floods in local nullahs/streams of Murree, Galiyat, Islamabad/Rawalpindi..."*
- *"In Gilgit-Baltistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the water flows will increase in rivers and nullahs due to high rate of snow and ice melting."*

#### Class 2: DIRECT RESTRICTION (`2`)
The sentence **explicitly describes travel or road access being compromised**—using words like *road closure*, *blockage*, *disruption to roads*, *slippery conditions causing road closure*, or *impassable routes*.
- *"Snowfall may disrupt roads in Murree, Galliyat, Naran, Kaghan, Dir, Swat, Kohistan..."*
- *"Rainfall may trigger landslides, mudslides or falling boulders potentially disrupting roads during this period in Upper Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Galiyat, Murree, Gilgit Baltistan..."*
- *"Landslides / mudslides may cause road blockage / closure in vulnerable hilly areas..."*

---

### 5. The Golden Decision Rule

> **THE LITMUS TEST:**  
> **Does this sentence talk about the weather/hazard event, or does it talk about what happens to roads and travel because of that event?**  
> - **Hazard happening $\rightarrow$ Class 1 (Potential Disruption)**  
> - **Roads / travel access affected $\rightarrow$ Class 2 (Direct Restriction)**

### 7. Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Don't label based on "scary" words:** Words like *"torrential"*, *"severe"*, *"avalanche"*, *"heavy downpour"*, or *"flash flood"* sound dangerous, but if roads/travel access are not explicitly mentioned as blocked or closed, it is **Class 1 (Potential Disruption)**.
2. **Don't extrapolate real-world common sense:** You might think, *"If there is a flash flood in a nullah, the mountain road must be flooded!"* While often true in reality, **label only what the text explicitly states**. Do not fill in assumptions that the advisory author did not write down.
3. **Don't let location lists confuse you:** A long list of 20 mountain districts (*"Chitral, Dir, Swat, Kalam, Kohistan, Shangla..."*) does not make a sentence a Direct Restriction. Look purely at the verb and hazard outcome.
4. **Don't let the source feeling official/urgent sway you:** Every sentence here is from an official hazard advisory — that is already true for 100% of the dataset, so it cannot be used to distinguish Class 1 from Class 2. Only the explicit road/access language can.

---

### 8. Golden Rule of Thumb

> **Label only what the text says—never guess at what probably happened on the ground.**  
> If you encounter a sentence that remains a genuine 50/50 tie even after applying the Litmus Test, assign your best-guess label and make a brief note in the **`label_notes`** column (e.g., *"Borderline between 1 and 2, leans 1 because road not explicitly mentioned"*).
